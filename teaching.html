Teaching

I have taught at the University of Chicago, where I taught five courses a year, at Trinity University, where I taught six, and at the University of Minnesota, where I led a common macroeconomics curriculum across eight instructors. I also taught a Ph.D. course at Collegio Internazionale Ca' Foscari.

Courses taught

Except where marked co-taught, I designed and taught these courses, from determining the curriculum to creating the homework and exam problems, and grading them or overseeing their grading. I developed answer keys and grading rubrics for consistent weekly homework and exam grading across as many as nine teaching assistants.

Ph.D. (topical)

  • Risk and Benefits of Digitized Money and Assets, Collegio Internazionale Ca' Foscari.

    A course on the theoretical models that examine new risk channels from central bank digital currencies, stablecoins, and crypto markets. It assumes first-year graduate macroeconomics and no prior knowledge of crypto markets, banking, or monetary policy.

    Topics. Central bank digital currencies and bank stability; their macro-risk consequences; stablecoins and financial risk; open-economy currency competition; and crypto monetary theory in three frameworks, positive and normative. Each topic is taught through its core papers.

Master's (topical)

  • Cryptoasset Markets, Financial Mathematics, University of Chicago.

    A course for students with no prior knowledge of crypto. It covers the technology, the markets, and the players, and tests where the analysis tools of traditional finance work, or fail, in cryptoasset markets.

    Topics. Blockchain technology and history; centralized markets and the reliability of reported exchange data; spatial and triangular arbitrage; decentralized finance, automated market makers, and impermanent loss; crypto returns, factor exposure, and momentum; and the roles tokens play in projects. Guest speakers from industry.

    Assessment. Five data projects, most done in groups, including a trading strategy written as code to run on an exchange. Students post their code and data to a shared folder, so each learns from the others' work.

Undergraduate (core)

  • Principles of Macroeconomics, University of Chicago, Trinity University, and University of Minnesota.

    By way of theory and policy applications, covers the major domestic and international macroeconomic issues: the determination of income and output, inflation, unemployment, and growth; money, banking, and the Federal Reserve; federal spending, taxation, and deficits; and international trade, exchange rates, and the balance of payments. It assumes no prior economics and no mathematics beyond algebra.

    Topics. GDP and cross-country convergence; the Solow model and growth accounting; real and nominal GDP; inflation and aggregate demand; trade; unemployment and business cycles; the aggregate demand and supply model and schools of thought; fiscal policy, debt, and taxation; and monetary policy, interest rates, and inflation.

    Assessment. Homework, in-class quick-response exercises, and quizzes at Chicago; homework, midterms, and a final at Trinity. Topic summaries and practice problems written for the course.

    Syllabus

  • Principles of Microeconomics, Trinity University.
  • The Elements of Economic Analysis I, University of Chicago.

    Develops the economic theory of consumer choice: optimal choices given incomes, preferences, and prices, how they change, and measures of consumer welfare, used to evaluate taxes and subsidies. It assumes multivariable calculus.

    Topics. Scarcity constraints; preferences and indifference curves; utility maximization; duality; income and substitution effects and the Slutsky decomposition; three measures of the impact of a price change; income and time allocation; allocation across time; expected utility and insurance; general equilibrium of an endowment economy; and Pareto optimality and the social planner.

    Assessment. Homework, in-class quick-response exercises, and quizzes. Homework tests analysis rather than recall.

    Syllabus

  • The Elements of Economic Analysis II (Intermediate Microeconomics), University of Chicago.

    A continuation of Elements I: producer choice, markets with one or a few suppliers, factor markets and the distribution of income, and elementary general equilibrium and welfare economics.

    Topics. Production functions and costs; cost minimization and profit maximization; firm and industry supply; partial equilibrium and market interventions; general equilibrium of a production economy; the welfare theorems and the social planner; externalities; monopoly; game theory; Bertrand, Cournot, and Stackelberg oligopoly; and public goods and asymmetric information.

    Assessment. Homework, in-class quick-response exercises, and quizzes. Homework tests analysis rather than recall.

    Syllabus

  • The Elements of Economic Analysis III (Intermediate Macroeconomics), University of Chicago.

    An introduction to macroeconomic theory and policy that extends the earlier methods to dynamic general equilibrium models of an economy with firms, consumers, and governments. It includes beginning MATLAB programming.

    Topics. Savings, consumption, and the Euler equation; bond and equity markets; economic growth, the Solow model, the golden rule, and the neoclassical growth model; macroeconomic measurement; consumption-labor decisions and unemployment; business cycles and real business cycles; money, interest rates, and cash-in-advance; monetary policy and expectations; and seigniorage.

    Assessment. Homework, in-class quick-response exercises, and quizzes. Homework tests analysis rather than recall.

Undergraduate (topical)

  • International Monetary Systems, University of Chicago and Trinity University.

    Monetary policy across international markets, global banking markets, and optimum currency areas, and their effects on business cycles and economic development. Students analyze data and policy with simplified theoretical models. The course is writing intensive.

    Topics. Exchange rates and foreign-exchange arbitrage; covered and uncovered interest parity; money, interest, and exchange rates; twin deficits and balance-of-payments crises; saving, investment, and the current account, from the accounting identity to a micro-founded model; exchange-rate policy; and central bank digital currencies and cryptocurrencies.

    Assessment. Homework that tests analysis rather than recall, in-class quick-response exercises, tests, and policy reports. Each class meeting has its own topic summary and practice problems, written for the course.

    Syllabus

  • Economic Policy Analysis, University of Chicago.

    Uses the theoretical, numerical, and empirical methods of the core sequence to analyze macroeconomic policy across taxation, government debt, unemployment, and monetary policy. Its monetary topics start from the three functions of money. The course is calculus- and programming-based (MATLAB or Python).

    Topics. Taxation, optimal government debt, and social insurance; money as a medium of exchange, a store of value, and a unit of account; price misperceptions; seigniorage and government debt; monetary policy and interest rates; and the central bank's commitment problem.

    Assessment. Homework and three policy analysis briefs, two from set prompts and one on a fiscal or monetary policy of the student's choosing. All slides, handouts, prompts, and questions are written for the course.

    Syllabus

  • International Trade, Trinity University.

    The economic theory of international trade, trade restrictions, and policy, applied to real-life observations, with practice in reading published data and analysis.

    Topics. Measuring welfare; relative supply and demand; Ricardian, specific-factors, and Heckscher-Ohlin models; labor mobility; economies of scale and monopolistic competition; testing the theory; trade barriers; trade diversion and creation; the politics of trade agreements; import substitution and export-led growth; subsidies; and dumping.

    Assessment. Weekly homework, a two-part midterm, and a research paper built through weekly drafts, with the most favorable of four grading plans applied automatically.

Professional education

  • Macroeconomics, University of Chicago, as a visiting instructor, for participants who already hold an undergraduate degree.

    Introduces participants with minimal macroeconomic background to macroeconomic policy analysis: measurement, why theory is needed for macro policy, and growth, open-economy deficit sustainability, monetary and fiscal policy, unemployment, inflation, and business cycles.

    Topics. National accounts and data; long-run growth; the circular flow and the open economy; exchange rates and external debt; money and monetary policy; loanable funds and fiscal policy; inflation; unemployment; and business cycles.

    Assessment. Group in-class exercises and four group problem sets, using data from open repositories. AI tools are allowed when the tool and prompt are disclosed.

Writing on teaching

Peer-reviewed articles

  • "Measuring Academic Mindset in Economics Courses," with 25 co-authors. International Review of Economics Education, June 2026.

    Proposes an aggregate measure of academic mindset, covering relevance, belonging, and growth, and examines how students' demographic characteristics predict their mindset when they enter an economics course. Students who do not identify as male, and first-generation college students, start with a lower mindset, and the difference is larger in introductory courses than in upper-level courses.

    Data. 3,212 students in 45 courses taught by 35 instructors at 24 U.S. institutions.

    Read the paper

  • "Designing Effective Assessments in Economics Courses: Guiding Principles," The Journal of Economic Education, 2024.

    For new instructors: what pedagogical theory says about sound assessment design, how it applies to economics courses, and examples of assessments from the economics literature to try.

    Read the paper
